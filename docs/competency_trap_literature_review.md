# Competency Traps: A Comprehensive Literature Review

## 1. Introduction and Overview

The competency trap represents one of the most consequential paradoxes in organizational and cognitive science: the very competencies that enable success can become the seeds of failure when environmental conditions shift. This phenomenon manifests across levels of analysis—from individual problem-solving and expert performance, to group coordination and communication, to organizational strategy and technology adoption. Understanding competency traps requires integration across cognitive mechanisms, social coordination processes, organizational structures, and evolutionary dynamics.

This literature review synthesizes research across six major areas: (1) foundational organizational theories of competency traps, (2) cognitive mechanisms underlying individual expert inflexibility, (3) group and social dynamics in convention formation and entrenchment, (4) architectural and technological sources of competency traps, (5) methodological precedents for studying these phenomena experimentally, and (6) alternative explanations that challenge or qualify cognitive-based accounts.

The review is organized around a research project that has established the competency trap effect in a controlled collaborative image-labeling paradigm and is now dissecting the underlying mechanisms. This context shapes the emphasis: greater attention is paid to mechanisms that can be manipulated and measured in laboratory settings, and to prior work that has used experimental methods to isolate causal pathways.

## 2. Foundational Organizational Theories

### 2.1 The Exploration-Exploitation Tradeoff

The theoretical foundation for understanding competency traps rests on James G. March's (1991) seminal analysis of the exploration-exploitation distinction in organizational learning. March demonstrated through computational simulation that organizations face a fundamental tradeoff between exploring new competencies and exploiting existing ones. This is not merely a strategic choice but a structural consequence of how organizations learn. Exploitation—refining, improving, and deploying current competencies—yields faster, more reliable, and more proximate feedback. In contrast, exploration—developing new competencies for uncertain futures—returns uncertain, delayed, and sparse feedback. Because feedback shapes organizational adaptation, the structure of the learning environment systematically biases organizations toward exploitation, even when exploration would be globally optimal (March, 1991).

March's insight that this bias is not a correctable motivational error but rather an inherent structural feature of learning systems is crucial. It means that competency traps are not failures of will or vision, but predictable emergent properties of how organizations accumulate and use knowledge. More competent organizations—those with tighter feedback loops between action and improved outcomes—are paradoxically more at risk of becoming trapped in exploitative cycles because the positive feedback from competence becomes more immediate and more salient.

### 2.2 Organizational Learning and the Competency Trap Concept

Levitt and March (1988) synthesized behavioral organizational theory with models of routine-based action to provide a comprehensive framework for understanding organizational learning. Their explicit definition of the competency trap has become canonical: "organizations are drawn into competency traps by the positive local feedback associated with routines that are locally best but not globally optimal" (Levitt & March, 1988, p. 327). This definition captures the core paradox: a routine becomes entrenched precisely because it works well in the current context, but this local optimality can prevent recognition of situations where alternatives would be superior.

Levitt and March distinguished competency traps from other learning failures by emphasizing the role of positive feedback. They also introduced the concept of superstitious learning—attributing success to the wrong causes—as a mechanism that exacerbates competency traps. When an organization succeeds with a particular routine but misattributes the success to aspects of the routine that are actually irrelevant, the organization becomes even more committed to the routine's irrelevant features. This distinction between learning traps caused by valid positive feedback versus those caused by misattributed causation opened the door to more nuanced theorizing about when competency traps are truly structural versus when they reflect correctable errors in causal inference.

### 2.3 Three Forms of Myopia in Learning Systems

Levinthal and March (1993) extended the competency trap framework by identifying three structural forms of myopia inherent to learning systems, arguing that these myopias cannot be corrected through individual motivation because they are properties of learning systems themselves. Temporal myopia—a systematic bias toward near-term outcomes over distant consequences—arises because learning depends on feedback, and feedback from proximate actions is immediate while feedback from actions oriented toward distant futures is deferred. Spatial myopia—local focus on immediately available alternatives rather than distant possibilities—emerges because learning requires exploration, and exploration in the immediate vicinity of current competencies is more efficient than radical exploration.

Most critically, Levinthal and March identified failure myopia: a systematic tendency to attend to successes and overlook failures. Successful organizations, almost by definition, generate abundant feedback about their successful actions and sparse feedback about their failures. This creates an asymmetry in the information available to guide learning. The organization learns extensively about how to replicate its successes but may remain ignorant of critical failure modes that rarely occur under current conditions. When environmental change increases the frequency of those failure modes, the organization's competency becomes maladaptive precisely because it is optimized for success patterns that no longer reflect the environment.

Levinthal and March's (1993) framework suggests that competency traps are not pathologies of learning but normal operating properties of adaptive systems. The implication is unsettling: even organizations with sophisticated feedback systems, motivated managers, and intellectual resources remain vulnerable to competency traps. The answer is not to try harder to learn (which would deepen temporal, spatial, and failure myopia) but to maintain structural commitment to exploration—essentially to systematically "waste" resources on activities that have lower short-term return than exploitation.

### 2.4 Core Capabilities and Core Rigidities

Leonard-Barton (1992) examined the paradoxical relationship between core capabilities and core rigidities through a multi-case study of 20 new product development projects across five companies. She identified four dimensions along which capabilities can become rigidities: technical systems (the physical hardware and tools embedded in facilities), skills and knowledge of the workforce, managerial systems (measurement, compensation, resource allocation, communication structures), and values and norms. Each dimension offers both enabling and constraining possibilities.

The critical insight is that the same organizational feature that confers advantage in exploiting an existing paradigm becomes an obstacle when paradigm change is required. A manufacturing facility optimized for a particular production process represents deep competence but embodies assumptions about what matters in manufacturing. A workforce trained in specific techniques has developed expertise but has also developed mental models and problem-solving approaches that may be less applicable in new domains. Managerial systems designed to enforce consistency and control in known processes actively suppress the experimentation and variation required for innovation in new domains. Values that emphasize reliability, efficiency, and discipline in execution become obstacles to exploration, risk-taking, and failure tolerance.

Leonard-Barton's framework is particularly valuable because it disaggregates competency traps into distinct dimensions. This suggests that competency traps are not monolithic: an organization might simultaneously face capability advantages and rigidity constraints in different dimensions. A firm with excellent technical systems and knowledge for the old paradigm but flexible managerial systems might escape a trap that would ensnare a firm with brittle managerial structures. This dimension-specific analysis points toward potential interventions: perhaps rigidities in particular dimensions can be addressed without dismantling the competencies that generate competitive advantage.

## 3. Cognitive Mechanisms

### 3.1 The Einstellung Effect and Mechanization of Thought

The classical foundation for understanding individual cognitive competency traps rests on Luchins' (1942) water-jar experiments. Luchins demonstrated that when participants solved a series of problems using a complex mathematical formula, they persistently applied that formula even when simpler solutions became available. The phenomenon is striking: participants do not merely choose the complex method; they often fail to even notice that simpler alternatives exist, despite working with those alternatives implicitly. Luchins termed this "Einstellung" or "set"—the tendency for thought to mechanize, for a previously successful problem-solving approach to spring to mind automatically and foreclose consideration of alternatives.

Critically, Luchins found that the mechanization effect strengthens with more prior practice. This directly instantiates the paradox of competency traps: greater mastery of a method leads to stronger mechanization, not greater flexibility. The mechanism appears to be automatization: with practice, the steps of a problem-solving sequence become automatic, executed without conscious deliberation. This automatization normally conveys benefits (faster execution, reduced cognitive load), but it has the side effect of reducing openness to alternatives. The automatic solution activates before conscious reasoning can consider whether alternatives might be better.

### 3.2 Attentional Capture and the Eye-Tracking Evidence

Modern neuroscience and eye-tracking methods have provided fine-grained evidence for the attentional mechanisms underlying the Einstellung effect. Bilalic, McLeod, and Gobet (2008a) conducted eye-tracking studies with chess experts and found that when presented with problems containing both a familiar solution (one they had encountered before) and a better solution, expert players' eyes returned repeatedly to the board positions associated with the familiar solution. Even after being explicitly told that a better solution existed, experts' attention continued to be captured by the familiar one.

This is not a problem of knowledge or capability. The experts demonstrably know the better solution; they can articulate it. The problem is that their attention is drawn to the familiar alternative in a manner that seems partially involuntary. The familiar pattern activates the associated solution representation, and this activation competes for attentional resources. Detecting and engaging with the alternative solution requires inhibiting this automatic activation, and the detection process itself requires attentional resources that are being consumed by the familiar pattern (Bilalic, McLeod, & Gobet, 2010).

Bilalic, McLeod, and Gobet (2008b) further demonstrated that higher-rated chess players are more susceptible to the Einstellung effect in certain problem structures. This is a critical finding: it directly contradicts the intuition that expertise conveys flexibility. Rather, expertise in a domain with particular problem structures can impair problem-solving when novel problem structures are encountered. The causality runs from competence to inflexibility, not the reverse. Experts are not inflexible in general; they are inflexible in ways that are directly tied to the domains in which they are most competent.

### 3.3 Cognitive Entrenchment and Knowledge Organization

Dane (2010) proposed cognitive entrenchment as a distinct psychological construct underlying expert inflexibility. Entrenchment refers to the tendency for domain schemas to become highly stable and resistant to revision, progressively narrowing the breadth of perspectives an expert entertains. Importantly, Dane distinguished cognitive entrenchment from rigidity: rigidity is often content-specific (a rigid belief about a particular domain fact), whereas entrenchment is a structural property of how knowledge is organized. An entrenched expert may not be rigid about individual beliefs but rather entrapped by a framework that makes certain kinds of thinking difficult.

Entrenchment predicts a particular type of problem situation should prove most difficult for experts: situations where the expert can consciously recognize that change is needed (so motivation and intention are not issues) but where changing the fundamental organizing framework of their knowledge is required. In such situations, the expert's superior knowledge paradoxically becomes a liability: the more extensive and well-integrated their knowledge network, the more disruptive any change to fundamental organizing principles becomes.

### 3.4 Domain Knowledge as Constraint on Representation

Wiley (1998) demonstrated experimentally that domain expertise can impair insight problem-solving when the domain knowledge biases problem representation. In one study, baseball knowledge either facilitated or impaired performance depending on whether the insight problem's elements evoked baseball-related meanings. When problem elements had baseball interpretations, experts' superior baseball knowledge actually made the problem harder to solve. The domain knowledge led them to impose baseball-related meanings on the problem components, and these meanings blocked the non-baseball representation needed for solution.

This work shows that the competency trap emerges not from deficiency of knowledge but from how knowledge is accessed and applied. Expertise creates expectations about what problem categories look like and what solutions are likely. When a problem appears to match a familiar category, the knowledge associated with that category is automatically activated. This automatic activation is normally beneficial (it speeds problem-solving in familiar domains), but it becomes costly when the problem actually belongs to a different category than its surface features suggest.

### 3.5 The Curse of Knowledge

Camerer, Loewenstein, and Weber (1989) identified the "curse of knowledge"—the phenomenon that once a person knows something, they cannot accurately imagine not knowing it. They demonstrated this in asset market experiments where informed traders systematically fail to anticipate that their uninformed counterparts lack information, leading to predictable pricing errors. The curse is not a lack of intelligence; it is a feature of how memory works. Information that is in mind dominates awareness; information that is absent from awareness becomes, subjectively, very hard to imagine.

Hinds (1999) extended this to expert performance, showing that experts are worse than intermediates at predicting how long novices will take to learn a task or at teaching novices effectively. The expert has internalized domain knowledge to such an extent that the component steps become invisible; the expert cannot remember what it felt like not to know these steps. When teaching or trying to help a novice, the expert's superior knowledge actually becomes a liability because it creates an empathy gap.

For competency traps, the curse of knowledge is particularly relevant when the environment changes in ways that novices immediately recognize but experts do not. A novice in a changed environment approaches it without the prior knowledge that would make the change seem invisible. An expert, attempting to interpret the changed environment through existing knowledge, may be unable to fully appreciate how much the environment has shifted because they cannot imagine the novice's perspective on it. They cannot unsee the patterns they have learned to see.

### 3.6 Predictive Processing and Strong Priors

Recent work in predictive processing (Clark, 2013) provides a neuroscientific framework for understanding why competency traps are deep features of how minds work. The predictive processing account suggests that the brain is fundamentally a prediction machine that minimizes prediction error by maintaining generative models of the world. These models are hierarchical: higher-level models generate predictions about lower-level sensory input. Learning updates the model parameters (the strength of beliefs) to reduce prediction errors.

In this framework, expertise corresponds to strong priors—high-confidence predictions about what the world is likely to contain. Strong priors are, under stable conditions, deeply beneficial: they reduce the amount of evidence needed to recognize familiar patterns, they focus attention efficiently, and they support rapid decision-making. But strong priors also create a problem: prediction errors from weak priors (about unexpected events) will be suppressed in favor of maintaining strong priors about expected events. The system does not equally weight all prediction errors; it weights them by the confidence of the prior model. Competent actors with strong priors will be relatively blind to weak evidence that the environment has changed, because this evidence generates weak prediction errors relative to the strong priors maintained by expertise.

### 3.7 Kind versus Wicked Learning Environments

Hogarth, Lejarraga, and Soyer (2015) distinguished between "kind" and "wicked" learning environments. In kind learning environments, feedback is fast, accurate, and complete, allowing genuine expertise to develop. Chess is the canonical example of a kind environment: moves have immediate consequences, the consequences are objective, and a player can accumulate vast experience. In wicked learning environments, feedback is slow, inaccurate, or incomplete, making genuine learning much more difficult. Many real-world domains—clinical medicine, business strategy, parenting—are predominantly wicked.

This distinction is crucial for competency traps because it suggests that the extent to which competency becomes entrapping depends on the structure of feedback in the domain. A deeply experienced physician, operating in a wicked learning environment with slow and often ambiguous feedback about diagnostic accuracy, may develop strong confident beliefs that are only tenuously grounded in actual diagnostic success. When the environment changes (a new disease emerges, treatment options shift), the confidence that served well in the old environment becomes a barrier to updating. In contrast, a chess master's competencies, developed in a kind environment with immediate accurate feedback, may be more readily updatable because the feedback structure has not allowed false confidence to accumulate.

### 3.8 Premature Closure and Diagnostic Error

Croskerry (2002) reviewed cognitive errors in medical diagnosis and identified premature closure as one of the most consequential. Premature closure occurs when a diagnostician identifies a plausible explanation for a patient's symptoms, accepts it, and then fails to consider disconfirming evidence. The mechanism is schema-driven: pattern recognition (a key component of expertise) identifies a match between the patient presentation and a known disease pattern, activating the schema for that disease. Once activated, the schema guides attention: the physician attends to features consistent with the schema and minimally attends to features inconsistent with it.

The interesting aspect is that premature closure is not a sign of lack of expertise. Rather, it is a consequence of the same pattern-recognition machinery that makes experienced physicians generally more accurate than novices. In common presentations, rapid pattern recognition enables quick correct diagnosis. In unusual presentations—a common disease presenting unusually, or a rare disease presenting ambiguously—this same machinery causes error. The competency trap in diagnosis is a direct consequence of how pattern recognition works: the better the expert's patterns, the faster diagnosis occurs, but the faster diagnosis, the less time and attention is devoted to considering alternatives.

## 4. Group and Social Coordination Mechanisms

### 4.1 Conceptual Pacts and the Formation of Shared Conventions

The study of how groups develop shared communication conventions provides essential grounding for understanding competency traps at the collaborative level. Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs (1986) conducted a foundational series of reference games in which pairs of participants needed to communicate about abstract figures. Over repeated trials, partners developed increasingly efficient, idiosyncratic referring expressions—for example, naming a complex figure "the Chevy" or "the airplane" rather than describing its features.

Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs termed the result "conceptual pacts"—mutual agreements that a particular expression refers to a particular entity. The key insight is that these pacts are not merely convenient shortcuts; they are collaborative accomplishments that require grounding (mutual acknowledgment that the reference succeeded) through the conversation. Once established, conceptual pacts become part of the common ground—the shared knowledge that partners assume the other possesses.

Crucially, conceptual pacts are partner-specific. Brennan and Clark (1996) demonstrated that when a new partner joins an established pair, the efficient labels developed with the original partner become less usable and sometimes become obstacles. Partners must work harder to establish new pacts with the third party, and the old pacts sometimes create misunderstandings when the third party interprets them differently. This reveals that what looks like "shared vocabulary" is actually a partner-specific agreement tied to particular conversational history.

### 4.2 Grounding and Common Ground

Clark and Brennan (1991) formalized the grounding process: each contribution to a conversation must be mutually accepted (grounded) before partners move forward. Grounding occurs through implicit or explicit acknowledgment that the contribution was understood. As conversations progress, common ground—the shared assumptions partners use to interpret each other—accumulates. Common ground is essential: it enables efficient communication and coordination. But common ground is also a constraint. As it becomes more extensive and more specific to the particular dyad's history, updating or revising it becomes more costly. Partners cannot easily revert to more explicit communication or challenge fundamental assumptions because doing so would destabilize the entire interactive system that has been built on those assumptions.

For competency traps, common ground represents a form of group-level entrenchment. The pair has developed efficient coordination by establishing what they mutually assume, and these mutual assumptions make their current coordination strategy locally optimal. However, these same assumptions become barriers when environmental change requires the pair to adopt a fundamentally different strategy. Updating common ground is not a matter of individual learning; it requires joint negotiation and mutual agreement to revise shared assumptions.

### 4.3 Convention Formation and Stability

Garrod and Doherty (1994) studied how communication conventions develop within and across groups. They found that when people interact within stable groups, strong group-specific conventions emerge. When people interact across groups (with different partners repeatedly), more abstract, generalizable conventions develop. This demonstrates convention formation as a social phenomenon: the conventions that emerge reflect the interaction structure of the group and are not simply individual solutions to communication problems.

Lewis's (1969) philosophical analysis of conventions provides the game-theoretic foundation: a convention is a regularity in behavior such that everyone conforms, expects everyone to conform, and has good reason to conform because others do. The self-reinforcing nature of conventions makes them stable: once established, conventions persist because abandoning them would disrupt coordination. Even if an alternative convention would be superior (by some objective measure), the established convention is self-perpetuating. Switching to an alternative would require coordination failure during the transition period.

Young's (1993) formal model of convention evolution demonstrated mathematically that once conventions are established, they are "stochastically stable"—small perturbations do not dislodge them. The more successful a convention has been (the higher the payoff it generates), the more stable it is and the greater the perturbation needed to disrupt it. This is another instantiation of the competency trap at the group level: the success of a convention entrenches it, making change even more difficult.

### 4.4 The Problem of Coordination Failure

Van Huyck, Battalio, and Beil (1990) demonstrated experimentally that groups playing coordination games frequently get trapped in Pareto-inferior equilibria—outcomes that are worse for all parties than available alternatives. The mechanism is strategic uncertainty: each player prefers to coordinate on the better equilibrium but is uncertain whether others will move toward it. This uncertainty is not irrational; it is an accurate reflection of the difficulty of coordination in the absence of explicit communication or prior agreement. Groups that have succeeded with an inferior convention face a critical problem: moving to a superior one requires that all members change simultaneously. The coordination failure occurs because no individual member wants to switch alone (risking being stranded in a worse-off uncoordinated state).

This mechanism is particularly important for understanding competency traps in collaborative settings. Even if all members of a group recognize that a new strategy would be better, they face coordination failure: each member would prefer that others switch first, ensuring that the group moves together to the new equilibrium. The equilibrium coordination problem can be just as binding as individual cognitive inflexibility.

### 4.5 Information Pooling Failures and Group Cognition

Stasser and Titus (1985) demonstrated that groups systematically fail to pool unshared information when making decisions. Members preferentially discuss information that all of them already know, rather than information unique to individual members. This creates a group-level competency trap: information that would signal that the group's current approach is becoming maladaptive is disproportionately held by individuals and not brought into the collective discussion.

Wegner (1987) developed the concept of transactive memory systems—the division of cognitive labor within groups where each member specializes in remembering certain domains and is relied upon by others for those domains. Transactive memory systems are efficient but fragile: they depend critically on knowing who knows what. When environmental change requires expertise in new domains, groups may have no member (or members scattered across the organization) who "knows" the relevant domain because expertise was distributed in relation to the old environment.

### 4.6 Automatic Alignment and Shared Mental Models

Pickering and Garrod (2004) proposed the interactive alignment model, showing that in dialogue, partners automatically align their representations at multiple levels: phonological (how to pronounce things), lexical (which words mean what), syntactic (how to structure sentences), and situational (what the conversation is about). This alignment is largely automatic and implicit, not the result of strategic effort. At the situational model level, aligned representations constitute shared mental models.

The automatic nature of alignment means that shared schemas form rapidly and often without explicit negotiation. Partners do not choose to align; they do so automatically as a consequence of coordinated interaction. This has implications for competency traps: shared schemas become entrenched not through explicit commitment but through the automatic, implicit alignment that occurs in coordinated interaction. Changing the shared schema requires not just individual learning but disruption of the automatic alignment processes that maintain it.

## 5. Architectural and Technological Sources of Competency Traps

### 5.1 Component Knowledge versus Architectural Knowledge

Henderson and Clark (1990) distinguished between two types of organizational knowledge: component knowledge and architectural knowledge. Component knowledge consists of understanding how individual parts work—the physics of a motor, the properties of a material, the algorithm for a subroutine. Architectural knowledge consists of understanding how components fit together—the overall design logic, the relationships between parts, the principles governing how components interact.

The critical insight is that these two types of knowledge are embedded in different locations within organizations. Component knowledge tends to be held by specialists and documented in manuals and designs. Architectural knowledge, in contrast, is often embedded invisibly in organizational structures, communication channels, problem-solving routines, and implicit assumptions about how work gets done. Architectural knowledge is so embedded in "the way we do things" that it is often invisible to members of the organization.

This framework enables precise prediction of when competency traps are most severe. When the environment demands what Henderson and Clark call "architectural innovation"—reconfiguring how components relate without changing the components themselves—established firms face catastrophic difficulty. Their component knowledge, which has been honed to excellence, remains valid and valuable. But the architectural knowledge that made that component knowledge effective has become obsolete, and because this knowledge is embedded in structures and practices, it is extremely difficult to recognize and change. The firm's greatest strengths become its blindest spots.

### 5.2 Technological Discontinuities and Competence-Destroying Change

Tushman and Anderson (1986) distinguished between competence-enhancing and competence-destroying technological discontinuities. Competence-enhancing discontinuities build on existing technological and organizational knowledge; they reward incumbents and disadvantage new entrants. Competence-destroying discontinuities render existing technological and organizational knowledge largely obsolete; they favor new entrants and disadvantage incumbents.

This distinction is crucial for understanding when competency traps are most pronounced. The competency trap is not equally activated by all environmental change. When change is competence-enhancing, the incumbent's competence becomes an advantage, and established routines serve well. When change is competence-destroying, the incumbent's competence becomes a severe liability because it has embedded architectural assumptions, created sunk costs in equipment and facilities, and shaped the skills and mental models of the workforce—all now pointing in the wrong direction.

Tushman and Anderson (1986) provided quantitative evidence from studies of three industries (cement, airline, minicomputer), showing that the probability of firm survival and continued success was significantly lower in the face of competence-destroying discontinuities. Importantly, this was not because of complacency or lack of effort by incumbent firms, but because genuine competence-destroying change requires essentially starting over—developing new component and architectural knowledge simultaneously.

### 5.3 Case Evidence: Polaroid and Digital Imaging

Tripsas and Gavetti (2000) conducted a detailed case study of Polaroid's failure to successfully transition to digital imaging. This is particularly valuable because Tripsas and Gavetti went beyond simply documenting the failure and instead traced the cognitive and organizational mechanisms. They distinguished between capability inertia (the organization cannot do the new thing) and cognitive inertia (the organization's mental models do not recognize that it needs to do the new thing).

Polaroid had tremendous capabilities in chemical imaging; the organization was excellent at what it did. However, Polaroid's executives developed mental models of their business rooted in the film paradigm. When digital imaging emerged, these mental models shaped how executives interpreted digital technology signals. Executives attended to information confirming that digital images would be lower quality (true at the time) and relatively ignored information suggesting that digital would eventually dominate (also true). Search behavior was systematically biased: the organization investigated how to improve digital image quality but did not investigate the possibility that customers' preferences might change and that they might prefer the convenience and immediacy of digital imaging despite lower quality.

Tripsas and Gavetti's process tracing shows that the cognitive structures that made Polaroid excellent at film photography—the mental models, the established interpretive frameworks, the schemas for evaluating technologies—actively misdirected organizational search and attention when the relevant environment changed. This is competency trap not as passive inability but as active misdirection by the same knowledge structures that drive excellent performance in the original domain.

## 6. Mechanisms and Alternative Explanations

### 6.1 The Selection Argument: Structural Inertia as Ecological Adaptation

Hannan and Freeman (1977, 1984) argued for a fundamentally different interpretation of organizational inertia. Rather than viewing inertia as a pathology (an organization being unable to adapt), they argued that inertia is a feature selected for by the environment. Organizations that are highly inert—that maintain stable structures, reliable procedures, and consistent strategies—survive better than highly changeable organizations. The environment rewards reliability and consistency (customers, investors, and partners prefer to work with stable partners who are accountable and predictable). Therefore, the organizations that survive to be observed by researchers are disproportionately the inert ones.

This selection argument is a powerful alternative to cognitive/mechanical explanations of competency traps. It suggests that what looks like organizational inflexibility might actually be evidence of ecological success: the organization has been selected to be inert because inertia conveys survival advantage. In this view, competency traps are not failures of learning or cognition but ecologically rational properties of organizations that have succeeded.

The selection argument is less applicable to laboratory settings with random assignment. In experimental research, participants are not selected for inertia; the inertia observed is not due to selection but to the mechanisms activated by success and learning. However, the selection argument remains important as a reminder that at the organizational level, field evidence for competency traps must rule out the possibility that observed inertia reflects selection rather than mechanism.

### 6.2 Sunk Cost and Commitment

Arkes and Blumer (1985) demonstrated the sunk cost fallacy in controlled experiments: people continue investing in losing courses of action because of prior investment. The sunk cost fallacy is a clear alternative explanation for persistence in prior strategies: people might not be trapped by competence but rather by having already invested resources in the current strategy. The presence of investment creates psychological commitment (because abandoning the investment feels wasteful) independent of whether the current strategy is competence-based.

This alternative is particularly important because competency-based entrenchment and sunk-cost commitment are difficult to disentangle in field settings. An organization might persist with a strategy because it has developed competence with it (true mechanistic entrapment) or because it has invested heavily in it (commitment-based persistence). In laboratory settings, one advantage is that both groups (successful and unsuccessful) can be equated on prior investment; the difference is in the nature of success. This allows distinguishing sunk cost commitment from competence-based entrenchment.

### 6.3 Threat Rigidity Versus Success-Based Rigidity

Staw, Sandelands, and Dutton (1981) reviewed evidence for "threat rigidity"—the tendency of organizations and individuals under threat to restrict information processing, centralize control, and increase reliance on well-learned responses. This is an alternative explanation: observed rigidity following success might actually reflect threat. Success might seem to create confidence and complacency, but as soon as subtle environmental signals of threat appear (the beginning of competitive erosion, the emergence of alternative technologies), organizations respond with threat rigidity. Observed inflexibility might reflect threat response rather than competence-based entrenchment.

This alternative is important because in natural settings, the moment of environmental change is often also a moment of threat. The organization that has been successful suddenly faces signals that its success is at risk. Are the observed rigidities due to competence-based mechanisms or threat-response mechanisms? Experimentally, one can manipulate threat independent of competence formation and measure which drives rigidity. If threat is removed while keeping competence constant, does rigidity persist? This allows distinguishing these mechanisms.

### 6.4 Rational Risk Aversion and Aspiration Levels

Greve (1998) argued from behavioral organizational theory that what looks like strategic inertia might actually reflect rational risk aversion. Organizations that have achieved success reach or exceed their performance aspirations. Once above the aspiration level, organizations become risk-averse (in prospect theory terms, moving from the loss domain back toward the reference point). Risk-averse organizations naturally choose to persist with successful strategies rather than risk disruption by trying new ones. This is not entrapment; it is rational risk management.

This alternative predicts that organizations with higher current performance (and thus further above their aspiration levels) should show more strategy persistence. The mechanism is not competence-based entrenchment but rather rational response to risk. Organizationally, this can be tested by measuring both aspiration levels and strategy persistence and examining their association.

### 6.5 Attention-Based Theory and Mediated Feedback

Ocasio (1997) proposed that organizations attend to and act upon limited domains; attention is a scarce resource channeled by firm-level structures. Environmental change might not trigger adaptation not because organizations are trapped by competence but because the change does not capture organizational attention. Attention is mediated by organizational structures (hierarchies, committees, reporting relationships) and organizational agendas (standing issues, priorities, crises). Change that does not fit within the organization's agenda or capture the attention of key decision-makers will not drive adaptation, even if the change would be recognized as important if attention were directed to it.

This is a powerful alternative because it suggests that the primary problem is not competence-based entrenchment but attention management. Organizations with more sophisticated environmental scanning systems and broader attention should escape competency traps more readily than those with narrow attention, independent of competence mechanisms.

### 6.6 Confirmation Bias as Universal Tendency

Nickerson (1998) reviewed evidence for confirmation bias—the universal human tendency to seek, recall, and interpret information in ways that confirm prior beliefs. This is an important alternative because confirmation bias is not specific to experts or competent individuals; it is a feature of human cognition generally. If competency traps are due to confirmation bias, then we should observe competency traps in novices as well as experts.

The distinction is crucial: if competency traps arise specifically from expertise (as the competence-based theories suggest), then they should show different patterns and magnitudes in experts versus novices. If they arise from confirmation bias (which affects everyone), then the patterns should be similar across expertise levels. This points toward experimental designs that compare how competent versus incompetent individuals respond to environmental change.

## 7. Experimental Methodologies for Studying Competency Traps

### 7.1 The Reference Game Paradigm

Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs (1986) and subsequent work in reference games (Garrod & Doherty, 1994; Hawkins et al., 2020) established the paradigm of having pairs of participants communicate about novel stimuli, with feedback about communicative success. Over repeated trials, efficient communication conventions emerge. This paradigm is particularly valuable for studying competency traps because it allows controlled study of how conventions form and what happens when conditions change.

The methodological strengths are significant: conventions form naturally without explicit instruction, the measure of "competence" is objective (communicative efficiency), and the formation process is observable (latency decreases, descriptions shorten). The paradigm allows researchers to manipulate: the difficulty of the initial communication problem, the feedback provided about success, the properties of the stimuli initially encountered versus those encountered after environmental change.

Metzing and Brennan (2003) extended this paradigm directly to the competency trap question by studying what happens when partners establish a convention and then one partner is replaced or the referent conventions apply to changes. This work provides a direct precedent for studying how established conventions resist change.

### 7.2 The Business Simulation Approach

Audia, Locke, and Smith (2000) created a controlled laboratory environment in which participants managed a simulated business. The strength of this approach is that it creates an environment with explicit rules, well-defined success metrics, and the capacity to shift environmental parameters. In their experiment, participants played the simulation, received success or failure feedback (manipulated regardless of actual performance), then the environment changed. Participants who had received success feedback persisted longer with their original strategy.

The mechanism proposed was that success leads to overconfidence and satisfaction, reducing the motivation to seek new information and adjust strategy. This approach allows experimental manipulation of success (independent of actual competence) and direct measurement of strategy persistence following environmental change.

### 7.3 Microworld Paradigms

Brehmer (1992) and Dörner (1996) developed microworld paradigms—complex dynamic simulations where participants manage systems (fire-fighting, epidemiology, economic systems) whose rules can change during the experiment. The microworld paradigm creates environments where success under initial conditions can lead to failure when conditions change. These paradigms are valuable because they combine realism (complex systems with many interacting variables) with experimental control (rule changes can be instantaneous and specified exactly).

The key methodological innovation is that the transition itself becomes the event. Brehmer showed that performance drops sharply at rule changes, and recovery depends on whether participants have developed rigid versus flexible mental models of the system. Participants who understood deep principles of the system adapted more readily than those who had memorized specific responses.

### 7.4 The Task-Switching Paradigm

Task-switching research (Meiran, 1996; Monsell, 2003) measures the cost of switching between tasks—reaction time slows when tasks switch compared to when the same task repeats. Switch costs are larger when tasks are more similar (interference) and when the previous task was more practiced. This paradigm measures strategy-switching costs at a fine-grained level: not whether people switch strategies, but how much cognitive effort the switch requires.

For competency traps, this paradigm suggests a method for measuring the effort required to switch from an established strategy to an alternative. If competency-based entrenchment is real, switch costs should be larger for participants with more prior practice on the initial strategy.

### 7.5 Insight Problem-Solving

Knoblich, Ohlsson, Haider, and Rhenius (1999) studied insight problems (problems for which the standard approach fails and an alternative representation is required for solution). By controlling which standard approach is activated (through prior problem exposure), researchers can induce stuck states and measure what allows escape. This paradigm allows studying entrenchment at the level of problem representation: can people re-represent the problem, or is the initially activated representation "sticky"?

## 8. The Current Research Context

The research program under consideration improves on existing paradigms in several respects. First, it uses collaborative image-labeling as a domain where "competence" is operationally defined as efficiency in communication about visual referents. Unlike business simulations (which impose rules somewhat arbitrarily), collaborative labeling is a genuine task with ecological validity—it mirrors real-world communication about visual information.

Second, the first study has established the basic competency trap effect in controlled conditions. Pairs that develop efficient labeling conventions for initial images show persistence with those conventions even when labeling new images for which those conventions are suboptimal. This extends the Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs paradigm to directly test convention entrenchment following environmental change.

Third, the research explicitly aims to dissect mechanisms in the second study. The ability to manipulate the type of strategy pairs develop (perhaps comparing schema-based versus bottom-up strategies, or comparing strategies developed through different feedback structures) and then measuring the magnitude of entrenchment opens pathways to understanding which mechanisms are actually operating. This addresses a critical gap in the existing literature: most prior work shows that competency traps occur, but few studies manipulate the properties of the competence to understand how entrenchment varies.

The collaborative context is also valuable for addressing the gap between individual cognitive mechanisms and group/organizational phenomena. Individual cognitive mechanisms (attention capture, Einstellung) operate at the cognitive level, but their manifestation in groups depends on how cognitions are coordinated and shared. The collaborative image-labeling paradigm inherently involves both individual cognitive processes and group coordination processes (grounding, common ground, convention formation).

## 9. Gaps and Future Directions

Several important gaps remain in the competency trap literature that the current research is positioned to address:

First, most experimental research has either demonstrated competency traps indirectly (through their consequences) or has used paradigms somewhat distant from natural coordination. The current image-labeling paradigm directly measures convention formation and entrenchment in a naturalistic coordination task. This allows precise measurement of how entrenchment develops over time and how properties of initial success shape subsequent entrenchment.

Second, the existing literature offers many possible mechanisms (cognitive, social, organizational, ecological), but few studies have directly compared mechanisms or manipulated them independently. The second study's design, explicitly testing different mechanisms, promises to clarify which are actually operative and which are epiphenomenal. For example, comparing the entrenchment of conventions that differ in cognitive demand, group size, or success pattern could reveal which properties of the initial competence most strongly drive entrenchment.

Third, the literature on individual cognitive mechanisms (Einstellung, entrenchment, attention capture) and the literature on group coordination mechanisms (convention formation, common ground, transactive memory) have developed largely independently. The collaborative paradigm naturally integrates these levels: individual cognitive mechanisms and group coordination mechanisms both shape the emergence and entrenchment of collaborative conventions.

Fourth, most prior research has measured competency trap effects at a single point in time or over a limited time window. Longitudinal measurement of how conventions emerge, solidify, and either persist or change following environmental shifts would clarify the temporal dynamics of entrenchment.

Fifth, the literature offers limited guidance on interventions. While many mechanisms are known, few studies have tested whether targeted interventions can mitigate competency traps. If mechanisms are understood at a fine-grained level, targeted interventions (e.g., periodic explicit reflection on convention justification, systematic rotation of decision-makers, maintained exploration of alternatives) can be tested.

## 10. Conclusion

The competency trap phenomenon spans multiple levels of analysis and draws on multiple theoretical traditions. The foundational organizational theories (March, Levitt & March, Levinthal & March) established that competency traps are a predictable consequence of how learning works, not aberrations. The cognitive mechanisms literature (Luchins, Einstellung, entrenchment, predictive processing) explains how competent individuals become inflexible despite having the knowledge required for flexible performance. The group coordination literature (Clark, grounding, conventions, common ground) explains how shared coordination structures become binding constraints on group action even when joint recognition that change is needed exists.

The architectural and technological literature (Henderson & Clark, Tushman & Anderson, Tripsas & Gavetti) demonstrates that competency traps have material bases in technology, structures, and artifacts. The alternative explanations literature reminds us that inertia can arise from multiple sources (selection, threat, risk aversion, attention, confirmation bias) and that any explanation of competency traps must rule out these alternatives.

What emerges is a picture of competency traps as overdetermined phenomena: multiple mechanisms converge to entrench successful strategies. At the individual level, practiced strategies automate and become attention-capturing. At the cognitive level, expertise creates strong schemas and priors that resist updating. At the group level, successful coordination creates shared conventions and common ground that become binding commitments. At the organizational level, competencies become embedded in structures, technologies, and artifact that constrain action. At the evolutionary level, reliable organizations are selected for, further entrenching inertia.

This convergence of mechanisms suggests why competency traps are so robust empirically and so difficult to escape. It is not enough to have the intellectual knowledge that change is needed; doing so requires updating cognitive structures, renegotiating group agreements, restructuring organizational artifacts, and overcoming selection pressures that reward stability. The competency trap is not a failure of individual cognition or organizational learning but a deep feature of how success creates the conditions for future failure.

The research program under consideration directly addresses critical gaps in this literature by studying competency traps in a naturalistic coordination task, explicitly manipulating mechanisms to understand their relative importance, and measuring both the emergence and entrenchment of conventions with high temporal resolution. By integrating individual cognitive, group coordination, and organizational perspectives, this work promises to advance understanding of competency traps from demonstration of their existence to mechanistic understanding of their operation.
