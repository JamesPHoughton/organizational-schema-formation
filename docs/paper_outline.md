# The Competency Trap: Mechanisms and Interventions

## Abstract / Summary paragraph

Why do some organizations successfully adapt to changes in their technological, legal, or market environments while comparable organizations falter, even when they appear to possess the capabilities needed to thrive? Competency traps — instances where a learned capability becomes a liability after the environment changes — have been widely documented in organizational case studies, but the mechanisms underlying them remain unclear because real-world settings confound the effects of the capability itself with selection effects, sunk costs, stakeholder politics, identity commitments, capital lock-in, and dozens of other co-occurring factors. Our strategy is to bring the phenomenon into a controlled laboratory paradigm that preserves the essential structure of a competency trap — a collaboratively developed strategy that improves performance, an environmental shift that renders it maladaptive, and the opportunity to revise — while stripping away the organizational complexity that makes the causal structure unidentifiable in the field. By showing that the trap reliably occurs in this simplified setting (Study 1), we establish that the cognitive and coordination dynamics alone are sufficient to produce it; the organizational factors, while surely important amplifiers in practice, are not necessary. We then use exploratory analysis of how groups actually respond to the environmental shift to characterize the dominant failure mode — not blind persistence with the old approach, but incremental adaptation at a tactical level where strategic-level rethinking was needed — and use this to motivate a targeted intervention (Study 2) that directly manipulates the level of reflection, testing whether prompting strategic thinking reduces the trap relative to prompting tactical adjustment. Each study narrows the set of viable candidate mechanisms: Study 1 rules out roughly thirty alternative explanations by design; the exploratory analysis identifies which surviving mechanisms are most consistent with observed behavior; and Study 2 tests whether the level-of-thinking mechanism is operative by causally manipulating it while holding practice, shared history, and the environment constant.


## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The phenomenon (0.5 pages)

Competency traps as a widely recognized pattern in organizational life. Organizations develop capabilities that serve them well, then fail to adapt when the environment shifts — not despite their capabilities but seemingly because of them.

Key citations: Levitt & March (organizational learning and the competency trap), Leonard-Barton (core rigidities), Levinthal & March (myopia of learning), Tushman & Anderson (competence-destroying change), March (exploration vs exploitation), Christensen (innovator's dilemma as a variant).

Establish shared ground: the field broadly agrees this happens and that it matters. No controversy here.


### 1.2 The identification problem (1 page)

While the phenomenon is well documented, the mechanism remains unclear. Observed competency traps in organizational settings are consistent with multiple causal accounts, and the evidence base — predominantly case studies, historical analyses, and cross-sectional comparisons — cannot distinguish between them.

Illustrative families of alternative explanation (3-4 sentences each, with citations):

- **Selection:** Organizations that develop deep competencies may differ systematically from those that don't. The trap might be about who develops capabilities, not about what capabilities do to you. (Cite relevant selection-effects literature.)

- **Structural/environmental:** Sunk costs, capital commitments, stakeholder resistance, and co-evolved ecosystems may prevent adaptation regardless of any cognitive or strategic mechanism. The trap might be about what you've built around the strategy, not about the strategy itself. (Cite relevant org theory — resource dependence, asset specificity, etc.)

- **Cognitive:** The process of developing a strategy may itself constrain future adaptation through lock-in, interference, or detection failure. The trap might be in how you think. (Cite Einstellung / Luchins, functional fixedness / Duncker, cognitive entrenchment / Dane.)

- **Social/coordination:** Shared mental models and coordination conventions may become self-reinforcing in ways that resist revision. The trap might be between people, not inside any one person's head. (Cite common ground / Clark, shared mental models / Cannon-Bowers.)

The key sentence: A case study of Nokia's failure to adapt to smartphones is consistent with all four accounts simultaneously, and no amount of additional historical detail can adjudicate between them.

Reference to supplement: "We enumerate over forty candidate explanations in Table S1 and assess which our design can and cannot rule out."


### 1.3 What would it take to resolve this? (0.5 pages)

State the desiderata for a paradigm that could discriminate between explanations:

- Random assignment to strategy type (rules out selection)
- Identical environment for all conditions (rules out environmental confounds)
- No sunk costs, stakeholder politics, or identity investment (rules out organizational amplifiers)
- Observable behavior during the transition (lets you characterize failure modes)
- Manipulable features (lets you test specific mechanisms in follow-up studies)

The simplicity of such a paradigm is not a limitation — it is the identification strategy. If the trap occurs even when all the organizational complexity is stripped away, the cognitive and coordination dynamics alone are sufficient. The organizational factors, while surely important amplifiers in practice, are shown to be not necessary.

Overview of the full arc: Study 1 (existence proof), exploratory analysis (failure mode characterization), Study 2 (mechanism test via intervention).

Each study narrows the set of viable candidate mechanisms. Study 1 rules out ~30 alternatives by design. Introduce the paradigm at a high level: collaborative image-labeling task, schema vs non-schema conditions, environmental shift in a final round. Details deferred to methods.


## 2. The paradigm

### 2.1 Design rationale

Walk through the desiderata from 1.3 and show how each design choice satisfies one:

- Random assignment to schema vs non-schema condition -> rules out selection
- Both conditions develop working strategies in R1-R3 -> not a practice-vs-no-practice comparison
- The strategies differ in kind (compressed/abstract vs perceptual/item-by-item), not in effectiveness -> the comparison is between processes, not competence levels
- Same R4 environment for both conditions -> rules out environmental confounds
- Transient dyads, no identity investment, no sunk costs -> rules out organizational amplifiers
- Observable labels, recalls, and accuracy -> lets you characterize failure modes

### 2.2 Task description

The collaborative labeling task. R1a/R1b/R1c staging (2, 4, 8 images). R2-R3 with 8 images. The labeler-matcher structure. Time pressure. The schema-inducing vs non-schema-inducing image sets.

### 2.3 The environmental shift (R4)

New images that are similar enough that the schema seems applicable but different enough that it produces errors (duplicates, mismatches). Explain why this specific kind of change — deceptively similar — is the theoretically interesting case. (Connects to Christensen: the iPhone wasn't radical enough to scare Nokia away.)

### 2.4 Exclusion criteria and quality controls

Pre-registered exclusion rules. QC survey. Communication-problem flags. The engagement threshold (>2 blank recalls in any round).


## 3. Study 1: Existence proof

### 3.1 Hypotheses

Schema groups will underperform non-schema groups in R4, despite comparable performance in R1-R3. Pre-registered, directional.

### 3.2 Sample and procedures

N, recruitment, payment, batches, platform.

### 3.3 Results

Lead with the headline: R4 accuracy is significantly lower for schema groups.

Round-by-round trajectory: both groups climb to ceiling in R3, then schema groups drop in R4 while non-schema groups stay roughly flat. The shape of the trajectory IS the story.

Statistical tests: Mann-Whitney U on R4 accuracy, effect size, CI.

### 3.4 Ruling out alternatives

Address the obvious alternative explanations explicitly, with data:

- "Maybe schema groups were just less competent overall?" -> No, R3 accuracy is the same.
- "Maybe schema groups didn't try as hard in R4?" -> No, labeling time and engagement metrics are comparable.
- "Maybe schema groups had less practice with the task?" -> No, same number of rounds, same time on task.

Reference Table S1: "Of the forty-four candidate explanations enumerated in Table S1, Study 1's design rules out thirty by construction. The surviving candidates are the cognitive mechanisms (Family 6) and the group coordination mechanisms (Family 7)."


## 4. Exploratory analysis: How do groups actually respond?

### 4.1 Coding procedure

Hagay's coding of R4 labeling behavior for all schema groups. Two coders, blind to hypothesis. Categories of R4 behavior.

### 4.2 The five-level taxonomy of R4 responses

| R4 behavior | n | % of schema users | Mean accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blind persistence (unchanged R3 schema) | ~4 | ~14% | ~2 |
| Incremental: add a feature | ~15 | ~53% | ~5 |
| Incremental: substitute a feature | ~3 | ~11% | ~7 |
| Incremental: simplify | ~4 | ~14% | ~7.25 |
| Strategic switch to holistic descriptions | ~5 | ~15% | varies |
| Idiosyncratic (arbitrary labels) | ~3 | ~9% | ~3 |

(Exact numbers from Hagay's coding to be reconciled.)

### 4.3 The surprise finding

The dominant failure mode is NOT blind persistence. Only ~14% blindly applied the old schema. The vast majority (86%) recognized the schema needed to change. But most responded at the wrong level — making tactical adjustments to the schema (adding features, substituting elements) rather than questioning whether schematic thinking was still the right approach.

The tactical adapters who added complexity (the modal response, ~53%) performed poorly (~5/8) because the adapted schema introduced new coordination failures — disagreements about the new feature, unnoticed duplicates, mismatched applications. Those who simplified or substituted did much better (~7/8), suggesting that the direction of adaptation matters, not just whether adaptation occurs.

### 4.4 Correlational finding and its limitation

Groups that switched to holistic descriptions tended to outperform incremental adapters. But this is correlational — it could be that better groups are more likely to switch (selection), not that switching causes better outcomes. This motivates Study 2: can we causally test whether the level of thinking matters?

### 4.5 What survives as candidate mechanisms

The exploratory analysis narrows the surviving candidates further:

- **Detection failure** is a small part of the story (~14% of failures). Most groups detected the problem.
- **Lock-in at the tactical level** is the dominant pattern. Groups recognized the schema was maladaptive but responded with incremental fixes rather than strategic rethinking.
- **Coordination on the new approach** is visibly operating — many failures involve partners disagreeing on how to apply the adapted schema.
- **Practice deficit** remains confounded — we can't tell if tactical adapters failed because of their approach or because they lacked practice with alternatives.

This motivates an intervention that targets the level-of-thinking mechanism specifically, holding everything else constant.


## 5. Study 2: Does the level of reflection matter?

### 5.1 Rationale

The exploratory analysis suggests that the dominant failure mode is thinking at the wrong level — tactical adaptation where strategic rethinking was needed. But the correlational evidence can't establish this causally. Study 2 directly manipulates the level of reflection with a pre-R4 prompt, while holding practice, shared history, stimuli, and the environment constant.

### 5.2 Design

All groups are in the schema condition. Identical R1-R3 experience for everyone. Schemas form naturally.

Before R4, random assignment to one of two prompts, matched in length and tone:

- **Strategic prompt:** "Before you begin, discuss with your partner: is your overall labeling approach still the best one for these new images, or should you consider a completely different approach?"
- **Tactical prompt:** "Before you begin, discuss with your partner: are there any adjustments you should make to your labeling approach to handle these new images?"

R4: Same UV images for everyone.

The tactical prompt is not a do-nothing control — it actively encourages the failure mode documented in the exploratory analysis. It is genuinely good advice in a stable environment. The claim is that it becomes a trap when the environment has changed, because it keeps groups focused on execution rather than questioning the premise.

### 5.3 What this design controls

- Practice with the macro task: identical (same rounds)
- Practice with the specific strategy: identical (both groups built schemas)
- Shared ground with partner: identical (same R1-R3 history)
- Cognitive load of switching: similar (both groups face same time pressure)
- Stimuli and task difficulty: identical (same UV images)
- Demand effects: controlled (both groups get a prompt of similar length and tone)

The only difference is what level the prompt asks them to reflect on.

### 5.4 Manipulation check

Human-coded R4 schema-reliance score (0-8 per group). Prediction: strategic-prompt groups show higher rates of schema abandonment and strategic-level change; tactical-prompt groups show more incremental adaptation.

### 5.5 Primary outcome

R4 accuracy. One-sided Mann-Whitney U (strategic > tactical), or two-sided if we prefer to be conservative.

### 5.6 Interpretation under each outcome

**If the strategic prompt helps (higher accuracy, more abandonment):**
- The level-of-thinking mechanism is operative. The trap is at least partly about how groups frame the problem, not just about what skills they have.
- Practice deficit alone cannot explain the trap, because both groups had identical practice histories.
- Practical implication: strategic review processes (pre-mortems, "are we thinking about this right?" prompts) can help.

**If the strategic prompt doesn't help (no difference in accuracy or abandonment):**
- The trap is robust to conscious reframing. The surviving candidates are the ones the prompt can't touch — practice deficit, residual interference, coordination costs of building new shared ground.
- These are deeper, more structural mechanisms that awareness alone cannot overcome.
- Practical implication: you need structural interventions (cross-training, strategy rotation, forced experimentation), not just better thinking.

**If the strategic prompt helps abandonment but not accuracy:**
- Groups switch strategies when prompted but the switch doesn't help — they're starting from scratch without the practice or shared ground to execute the new approach.
- The trap has two layers: a framing layer (which the prompt fixes) and a capability layer (which it can't).
- Practical implication: strategic review is necessary but not sufficient; organizations also need to maintain alternative capabilities.

Each outcome further narrows the set of viable mechanisms.


## 6. General discussion

### 6.1 What we've learned

Summary of which candidate mechanisms have been ruled out (by Study 1's design), which are inconsistent with observed behavior (from the exploratory analysis), and which are supported or contradicted by Study 2's results.

### 6.2 The "toy example" response

The criticism that our paradigm is too simple to capture real-world competency traps has the logic backwards. If the trap only appeared in complex organizational settings, you could never know which factors produced it. By showing that it occurs in a 30-minute dyadic task with none of the organizational complexity, we establish that the cognitive and coordination dynamics alone are sufficient. The simplicity is the identification strategy.

We do not claim that sunk costs, stakeholder resistance, identity, and organizational politics are irrelevant to competency traps in practice. They almost certainly make the trap deeper, stickier, and harder to escape. But they are not necessary for it to occur. The trap has a cognitive core that operates even in their absence. Real-world competency traps are likely this cognitive core plus organizational amplifiers layered on top.

### 6.3 Implications for practice

Different mechanisms imply different interventions:

- If the bottleneck is detection: train monitoring, build feedback loops, schedule periodic environmental scans.
- If the bottleneck is level of thinking: implement strategic review processes, pre-mortems, "how would we know if our approach was wrong?" prompts.
- If the bottleneck is practice deficit: cross-train, maintain a portfolio of strategies, invest in exploration even when exploitation is working.
- If the bottleneck is coordination: build explicit strategy-discussion norms, create safe spaces for proposing radical alternatives, reduce the social cost of dissent.

Our studies begin to adjudicate which of these is most relevant. Study 2's result directly informs whether the "strategic review" class of intervention has purchase.

### 6.4 What's next

The four-mechanism taxonomy (detection failure, level-of-thinking lock-in, practice deficit, coordination cost) is itself a contribution. We've addressed two of the four. Future work:

- **Persistence-despite-recognition:** Would require a manipulation that hands recognition over explicitly (e.g., telling groups "your schema will produce duplicates") and tests whether they persist anyway. Deferred because of control-condition problems.
- **Residual interference:** Could be probed by content analysis of R4 labels — do groups that consciously abandon the schema still produce schema-influenced labels? Available in the current data as exploratory analysis.
- **Coordination cost:** Could be probed by comparing individual vs dyadic versions of the task, or by analyzing communication patterns during the transition. A natural Study 3.

### 6.5 Limitations

- Lab task, not a real organization. (Addressed in 6.2 — the simplicity is the identification strategy.)
- Dyads, not larger groups. (But most coordination happens in small groups; dyads are the minimal social unit where coordination mechanisms can operate.)
- Short timescale (~30 minutes). (A feature: rules out institutional lock-in, identity formation, and sunk-cost accumulation as necessary mechanisms.)
- Specific stimulus class. (A feature: can be precisely manipulated; future work can test generalizability across stimulus types.)
- The intervention wording is load-bearing. (Acknowledge that different phrasings might produce different results; report exact wording; future work can test robustness.)
